              How extended chords are built

              Extended chords keep doing what these wheels already do: stacking thirds. A triad is root–3–5. A seventh chord adds one more third on top (root–3–5–7). Keep going and you get the 9th, 11th and 13th. Those are just the 2nd, 4th and 6th of the scale, placed an octave higher. After the 13th, the next third lands back on the root, so a 13th chord contains all seven notes of the scale.

              Using C major as the parent scale:

              NoteCEGBDFA
              DegreeR35791113

              The naming follows three rules. A 9, 11 or 13 in the name implies the 7th is there too; if you add the 9 without the 7, it's called "add9" (Cadd9 = C E G D). The highest number named implies the ones below it, so C13 technically includes the 9 and 11. In practice, some of those get left out, for the reasons below.

              The triad-over-root idea extends naturally

              The maj7 wheel shows that a maj7 is a minor triad on the 3rd, over the root: Cmaj7 = Em/C. Keep stacking and the next layer is another triad:

              C | E G B | D F♯ A

              That is the root, then Em, then a D major triad: Cmaj13♯11. The D F♯ A triad on its own over C, written D/C, gives the 9, ♯11 and 13. This is a classic Lydian sound.

              The same trick works on dominant chords, where it's called an upper-structure triad. Here it is over G7 (G B D F):

              Triad on top of G7Tones it addsResult
              A major (A C♯ E)9, ♯11, 13G13♯11
              E major (E G♯ B)13, ♭9, 3G13♭9
              B♭ major (B♭ D F)♯9, 5, ♭7G7♯9
              E♭ major (E♭ G B♭)♭13, R, ♯9G7♯9♭13

              Pianists often voice these as the 3rd and 7th in the left hand (B and F) with the triad in the right hand. The table in the chord builder above does this for whatever root is selected.

              Which extensions fit which chord

              Not every extension sounds good on every chord. The main danger is a minor 9th interval (a half step plus an octave) above a chord tone, especially the 3rd. That's why the natural 11 on a major chord (F against E in Cmaj7) is treated as an avoid note, and ♯11 is used instead.

              Chord typeNatural choicesCommon alterationsNotes
              maj79, 13♯11The natural 11 clashes with the 3rd
              m79, 11, 13—The 13 implies the Dorian mode; use it on ii chords, not on iii
              Dominant 79, 13♭9, ♯9, ♯11, ♭13The most flexible chord; alterations add tension before resolving
              m7♭511, ♭139 (natural)The natural 9 comes from Locrian ♮2
              °79, 11, ♭13, 7—Tensions sit a whole step above each chord tone (whole-half diminished scale)
              sus4 dominant9, 13♭9The 11 replaces the 3rd, so there's no clash: G9sus4 = F/G

              Voicing: what to leave out

              A 13th chord has seven notes, and playing all of them sounds muddy. Drop notes in this order. First the 5th, unless it's altered. Then the root, since the bass player usually has it. Then the 11 on major and dominant 13 chords. Always keep the 3rd and 7th, the guide tones, because they define the chord quality. A playable G13, for example, is G in the bass with F B E above: the 7, 3 and 13.

              Reharmonization

              Reharmonizing means replacing or adding chords under a melody while keeping the music moving in the same direction. The one rule that overrides everything: the melody note has to work over the new chord, either as a chord tone or as a valid extension. Check each melody note against the table above.

              The main techniques, from simplest to boldest:

              1. Diatonic substitution. Chords that share notes can stand in for each other. The maj7 wheel shows this directly: Cmaj7 = Em/C, so Em7 shares three notes with Cmaj7, and Am7 (A C E G) shares three too. This gives three families. The tonic group (I, iii, vi) is Cmaj7, Em7 and Am7. The subdominant group (ii, IV) is Dm7 and Fmaj7. The dominant group (V, vii) is G7 and Bm7♭5. The m7 wheel works the same way: Dm7 = F/D, so Dm7 and Fmaj7 are near-twins.
              2. Adding extensions or alterations. This keeps the same function with a richer color, for example G7 → G13 or G7♯9. It's the least risky reharmonization.
              3. Secondary dominants. Put the V7 of any chord just before it. In C, A7 is the V of Dm7, so C – Dm – G – C becomes Cmaj7 – A7 – Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7.
              4. Inserting a ii–V. Place the related ii chord before any dominant, or turn any target chord into a ii–V destination. To approach Dm7, use Em7♭5 – A7 → Dm7. The 7–3–6–2–5–1 progression is a chain of this idea around the circle of 5ths.
              5. Tritone substitution. Replace a dominant with the dominant a tritone away: G7 → D♭7. They share the same two guide tones (B/C♭ and F), just swapped, so they pull toward the same resolution. The payoff is a chromatic bass line: Dm7 – D♭7 – Cmaj7.
              6. Modal interchange (borrowed chords). Borrow chords from the parallel minor key. In C major, common borrowed chords are Fm7 (the minor iv), B♭7 (the "backdoor" dominant) and A♭maj7. For example, Fmaj7 → Fm7 → Cmaj7 gives a bittersweet turn.
              7. Passing diminished chords. Fill a whole step with a diminished 7th: Cmaj7 – C♯°7 – Dm7. This is essentially an A7♭9 without its root.
              8. Pedal points and slash chords. Keep the bass note and change the harmony above it. This is exactly the wheel concept. Over a D pedal, you could move F/D → G/D → C/D (the last is Dm11 with the 3rd left out).

              A worked example

              A basic progression in C, reharmonized in layers. Press play on any row to hear it.

              LayerBar 1Bar 2Bar 3Bar 4Bar 5
              OriginalCmaj7Am7Dm7G7Cmaj7
              + ExtensionsCmaj9Am9Dm11G13Cmaj9
              + Secondary dominantCmaj9A7♭9Dm11G13Cmaj9
              + Tritone subsCmaj9E♭9Dm11D♭7♯11Cmaj9

              In the last version, the bass jumps from C up to E♭ and then slides down by half steps back home: E♭ – D – D♭ – C. The guide tones inside the chords also move by step or stay put. That smooth inner movement is what makes a reharmonization sound deliberate rather than random. A good habit is to trace the 3rds and 7ths from chord to chord; if each one moves by a half or whole step, the change will almost always sound right.
